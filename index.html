A thought paper for the airline industry

The case for agentic AI in fuel economics

Fuel is 20–35% of an airline's operating cost, and the decisions that shape it are split across six departments, including treasury, procurement, dispatch, operations, finance, and maintenance, that rarely talk to each other in real time. This is what an orchestration layer over that fragmentation could look like.

01. The assumption we need to drop

There is no single "fuel price" for an airline.

Ask most people how an airline buys fuel and you get one of two guesses, either a flat price locked at the start of the financial year, or a trading desk buying spot fuel like a hedge fund, and neither guess is right. Airlines run four overlapping decision horizons at once, and almost none of them are made once a year. This is why fuel dominates CASK, the cost per available seat kilometer that airlines actually manage flights against. A lower CASK means a more efficient and more profitable flight, and no line item moves CASK faster than fuel.

02. Where real-time thinking already exists

Tankering is the one decision airlines already make flight-by-flight.

If fuel is cheaper at the departure airport than the destination, an aircraft can carry extra fuel to avoid buying the expensive stuff later, trading a small weight penalty for a larger price saving. It's the clearest proof that real-time, flight-level fuel decisions are already operationally possible. The opportunity is extending that same logic everywhere else.

A $150/ton gap justifies carrying extra fuel out of Singapore, even after accounting for the burn penalty of flying heavier. Airlines already run this calculation per flight, and the rest of this paper asks why almost nothing else in fuel economics gets the same real-time treatment.

03. Who actually decides

Centralized risk, regional execution.

Network carriers (Lufthansa, Emirates, Singapore Airlines, United) run centralized treasury and hedging out of one desk, while physical fuel is still negotiated regionally, airport by airport. Domestic and LCC carriers (IndiGo, Southwest, Ryanair) collapse this into one team because their operations sit inside one geography. Either way, the gap between the centralized hedge view and the regional physical-buy view is exactly where coordination breaks down.

Network / global carrier

One hedge mandate, four+ regional procurement teams negotiating separately against it.

Domestic / LCC carrier

The same desk owns hedge and physical buy, which is easier to align, but it still runs on separate systems from ops.

04. Why this isn't already solved

Every team optimizes its own slice. Nobody owns the whole.

Airlines already run sophisticated optimization software for hedging, for routing, for crew, and for flight planning, and the problem is that each system optimizes locally, against its own KPI, with no shared signal to the others.

💹TreasuryPrice volatility, hedge effectiveness
📦ProcurementSupplier reliability, negotiated discounts
🛫OperationsOn-time departure performance
🧭DispatchFlight safety, minimum required fuel
🧾FinanceFuel budget vs. plan

nobody's KPI is "total network fuel economics"

06. The orchestration layer

Not one giant optimizer. A supervisor over specialists that already exist.

This layer works alongside treasury's hedge models and dispatch's flight-planning software, since those solvers already do their jobs well, and it watches all of them, knows when an assumption has changed, and coordinates a response across boundaries no single system currently crosses.

07. Why this has to be continuous

Most fuel decisions are made once. Conditions change six times before departure.

A 06:00 dispatch calculation rarely gets revisited by 09:00, even after a runway change, a thunderstorm, a cargo increase, or a crude move, because nobody owns the job of asking whether anything has changed enough to matter, every few minutes, across every function.

Observe → Reason

Ingest live prices, weather, loads, positions. Ask what changed since the last pass.

Simulate → Estimate

Run the affected decisions forward, quantify the $ delta of adjusting now vs. waiting.

Recommend → Execute → Learn

Route to a human approver or auto-execute within guardrails, then feed the outcome back in.

09. For the team, how we'd actually build this

Three phases, each one shippable and demo-able on its own.

This paper doubles as a build brief, and none of the three phases requires replacing an airline's existing optimization stack, since each sits alongside it, reads from it, and, only in phase 3, writes back into it.

PHASE 1

Advisory copilot

This phase is read-only, with agents ingesting market, weather, and ops feeds and surfacing cross-functional recommendations to a human dashboard, without any system-of-record writes.

  • Treasury + Procurement + Weather agents only
  • Explainable recommendation cards for a human to review
  • Proves the cross-agent reasoning is sound
GUARDRAIL. Human approves every action.
PHASE 2

Cross-agent recommendations

Add Operations, Fleet, and Route agents. Supervisor agent starts chaining recommendations (like the Sydney tankering example) with a full audit trail.

  • Full agent mesh from the architecture above
  • Decision Agent produces a $ impact estimate per call
  • Still human-approved, but at network scale
GUARDRAIL. Approval happens in batches across the network.
PHASE 3

Bounded autonomous execution

Low-risk, high-frequency decisions (e.g. minor tankering adjustments within pre-approved bands) execute automatically. Everything else still routes to a human.

  • Hard-coded guardrails on $ size and risk category
  • Full rollback and override at any point
  • Learning loop feeds back into Phase 1–2 models
GUARDRAIL. Autonomy stays within pre-approved bands.

The reframe

Today's systems answer, "what's the optimal uplift for this flight?"
An agentic layer answers, "what should the airline do differently, right now, given everything that changed in the last hour?"

That's the shift, from isolated optimization inside each department, to enterprise-wide decision orchestration across all of them. It's a coordination layer that was structurally missing all along.
